# Anti-patterns — Tim Ferriss

Everything the corpus warns against, with the reasoning and the failure each produces. Sources are named per entry.

## The deferred-life plan

**The pattern:** work brutally for decades on the promise of freedom "later," banking all leisure and living for a retirement at 65.
**Why it fails:** it bets your healthiest, most capable years on surviving to spend a lump sum; it ignores the compounding cost to health, relationships, and curiosity; and it is fragile to layoffs, illness, and changed circumstances. The corpus's antidote is lifestyle design and mini-retirements — distribute freedom across life.
**Source:** *The 4-Hour Workweek* (2007), "Definition."

## Busyness as productivity theater

**The pattern:** filling the day with motion and reachability, mistaking activity for progress.
**Why it fails:** "Being busy is a form of laziness — lazy thinking and indiscriminate action." Busyness is often avoidance of the hard, important few tasks, dressed up as diligence. It also signals a missing 80/20 analysis.
**Source:** *4HWW* (2007), "Elimination."

## Optimizing or automating before eliminating

**The pattern:** reaching for a tool, an automation, or a virtual assistant to handle a task more efficiently.
**Why it fails:** if the task should not exist, automating it just pays to multiply an inefficiency — "never automate something that can be eliminated, and never delegate something that can be automated." The high-leverage, most-skipped move is deletion.
**Source:** *4HWW* (2007), "Automation."

## Efficiency applied to the unimportant

**The pattern:** getting very good at doing a task that does not matter.
**Why it fails:** "Doing something unimportant well does not make it important," and "requiring a lot of time does not make a task important." Effectiveness (right things) must precede efficiency (things done well), or you accelerate in the wrong direction.
**Source:** *4HWW* (2007), "Elimination."

## Information overconsumption

**The pattern:** staying "informed" through constant news and non-actionable media.
**Why it fails:** most information is neither timely nor actionable; consuming it taxes attention, raises anxiety, and crowds out the few inputs that matter. The antidote is the low-information diet and selective ignorance — read/watch only what you can act on.
**Source:** *4HWW* (2007), "Elimination."

## Maximum dose instead of minimum effective dose

**The pattern:** assuming more is better — more training volume, more restriction, more hours, more medication.
**Why it fails:** past the MED, additional input produces no additional result and often causes harm (injury, burnout, plateau). "Boiled is boiled." The discipline is to find the least input that produces the outcome and stop.
**Source:** *The 4-Hour Body* (2010), "Fundamentals."

## The talent excuse

**The pattern:** attributing others' skill to innate talent and one's own failure to a lack of it.
**Why it fails:** it treats as fixed what is mostly a function of method and material selection. The corpus holds that most skills yield quickly to deconstruction and drilling the high-frequency 20% — so "I'm not talented" is usually a hidden claim about method, not ability.
**Source:** *The 4-Hour Chef* (2012), "Meta-Learning."

## Hero worship in choosing teachers

**The pattern:** learning from the most accomplished practitioner because they are the best *performer*.
**Why it fails:** elite performers often can't explain what they do (implicit knowledge, or natural advantages a beginner can't copy). A self-taught or mid-tier practitioner who can articulate simple, transferable technique may teach a beginner far faster. Pick the teacher for teaching leverage, not fame.
**Source:** *The 4-Hour Chef* (2012), "Meta-Learning."

## Deciding health by dogma instead of data

**The pattern:** accepting a diet, supplement, or training rule because it is conventional wisdom or an authority said so.
**Why it fails:** individual physiology varies enough that population advice is a hypothesis, not an answer. The antidote is n=1: run a small test, measure objectively (blood work, DEXA, tracked metrics), and let the data overrule the rule.
**Source:** *The 4-Hour Body* (2010).

## Waiting for the right time (perfectionism)

**The pattern:** postponing a meaningful move until conditions, skills, or savings are "ready."
**Why it fails:** "Conditions are never perfect. 'Someday' is a disease that will take your dreams to the grave with you… just do it and correct course along the way." Waiting for certainty forfeits the compounding value of starting and iterating.
**Source:** *4HWW* (2007), "Definition."

## Defining goals while leaving fears undefined

**The pattern:** setting ambitious goals while never examining the fears that actually block action.
**Why it fails:** the binding constraint is usually unnamed fear, and "we suffer more often in imagination than in reality." Goals without fear-setting leave the real obstacle intact; the vague dread stays larger than the (mostly preventable, mostly repairable) worst case.
**Source:** TED2017, "Why you should define your fears instead of your goals."

## Chasing passive income as effortless riches

**The pattern:** hearing "4-hour workweek" or "muse" as a promise of money with no work.
**Why it fails:** the corpus is about *elimination and engineering*, not effortlessness — a muse requires testing, setup, and systems, and the "4-hour" figure is the output of ruthless prioritization, not a starting condition. Reading it as get-rich-quick skips the work that makes the leverage real. (See also `disagreements.md` vs. Hormozi on active work.)
**Source:** *4HWW* (2007), "Automation"; author's framing.
